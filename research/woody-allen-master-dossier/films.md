## Concise Original Synopsis

A Japanese secret-agent thriller is re-dubbed with an entirely new comic script. The
redrawn plot has agent Phil Moskowitz hired by the ruler of a small nation to recover a
stolen recipe for the world's finest egg salad, pursued by rival criminals and two
sisters, Suki and Teri Yaki. Allen appears in newly shot framing material, explaining
the premise to camera, and the Lovin' Spoonful perform interpolated musical numbers. The
comedy comes entirely from the gap between the straight-faced action footage and the
absurd dialogue laid over it.

## Concise Original Synopsis

A mock documentary traces the career of Virgil Starkwell, an inept petty criminal from a
poor Brooklyn family. Interviews with parents, teachers, a psychiatrist and a former
cellmate are intercut with re-enactments of his failures: a cello player in a marching
band, a chain-gang escapee, a bank robber undone by illegible handwriting on his hold-up
note. He falls in love with Louise, a laundress, and tries intermittently to go straight.
The film is built as a parody of true-crime reportage, with a deadpan narrator supplying
the authority the images undercut.

Fielding Mellish, a timid New York products tester, falls for Nancy, a political

Marcos, a Latin American dictatorship, where he falls in with rebels in the hills and is
improbably carried to the leadership of the revolution. Returning to the United States,

## Concise Original Synopsis

Seven unconnected comic sketches, each prompted by a question from a popular sex-advice
book. A court jester tries an aphrodisiac on a queen; a respectable doctor falls in love
with a sheep; an Italian husband discovers his wife can only be aroused in public; a
suburban man is caught in his wife's clothes; a 1950s panel show quizzes contestants on
perversions; a mad scientist's experiment produces a giant escaped breast; and, in the
closing episode, a man's body is run as a mission-control operation by uniformed
technicians, with Allen cast as an anxious spermatozoon. The targets are both sexual
anxiety and the pseudo-clinical register of the source book.

## Story Setting

- **City/cities:** various; a medieval court, an Italian city, an American suburb, a rural laboratory, the interior of a human body
- **Period:** mixed — medieval, 1950s, contemporary
- **Important story locations:** the court; the television studio; Dr. Bernardo's laboratory

## Production Context

- Loosely licensed from David Reuben's bestselling 1969 book; almost none of the book's
  content survives beyond the segment titles.
- Opening and closing credits play over a mass of white rabbits.
- The Italian segment is an explicit homage to Antonioni and Fellini, shot in Italian with
  subtitles.
- The Dr. Bernardo segment parodies Ed Wood's *Bride of the Monster* and *The Unearthly*,
  both of which featured John Carradine.
- Commercially Allen's first substantial hit: over US$18m in North America against an
  approx. US$2m budget; tenth highest-grossing US release of 1972 `[CHECK]`.

## Themes

Sexual anxiety; parody of expertise; genre pastiche.

- **Jazz relevance:** Mundell Lowe was a jazz guitarist — an early instance of jazz

  device with no close precedent at the time.

## What's New Pussycat?

- **Title:** What's New Pussycat?
- **Year:** 1965
- **Format:** Theatrical feature
- **Director:** Clive Donner
- **Woody Allen role:** **Screenwriter** (his first produced screenplay); actor as Victor Shakapopulis
- **Principal collaborators:** Peter Sellers, Peter O'Toole, Romy Schneider, Capucine, Ursula Andress; producer Charles K. Feldman; title song by Burt Bacharach and Hal David, performed by Tom Jones
- **Concise description:** A farce set in Paris around a magazine editor pursued by women and his psychoanalyst, a chaotic lecher. Allen's screenplay was extensively reworked during production, a process he has described as formative in his decision to direct his own material.
- **Career significance:** **Very high.** The entry point to film. The official site's own filmography erroneously credits Allen as *Director* on this film [S002] `[CONFLICT]` — he did not direct it. The experience is the standard explanation for his insistence on control thereafter.
- **Sources:** [S002], [S020], [S022]

## Play It Again, Sam

- **Title:** Play It Again, Sam
- **Year:** 1972
- **Format:** Theatrical feature
- **Director:** Herbert Ross
- **Woody Allen role:** **Screenwriter** (adapting his own 1969 play); actor as Allan Felix
- **Principal collaborators:** Diane Keaton, Tony Roberts, Jerry Lacy (as Humphrey Bogart); producer Arthur P. Jacobs
- **Concise description:** A film critic abandoned by his wife is coached in seduction by an imagined Humphrey Bogart, and falls for his best friend's wife.
- **Career significance:** **High.** The film that established the Allen–Keaton–Roberts screen trio that *Annie Hall* and *Manhattan* would use. It is also the clearest demonstration of what an Allen script looked like directed by someone else — and is routinely misremembered as his own direction. The legacy filmography correctly lists him as Writer and Actor only [S002].
- **Sources:** [S002], [S020]

## The Front

- **Title:** The Front
- **Year:** 1976
- **Format:** Theatrical feature
- **Director:** Martin Ritt
- **Woody Allen role:** Actor — Howard Prince. He did not write it.
- **Principal collaborators:** Zero Mostel, Herschel Bernardi, Michael Murphy; screenwriter Walter Bernstein
- **Concise description:** During the Hollywood blacklist, a cashier agrees to put his name to scripts by blacklisted writers and is drawn into the hearings himself.
